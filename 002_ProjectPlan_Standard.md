## Definition

A project plan is a document that lays out exactly what you're trying to accomplish and how you're going to do it.

At its core, it answers these basic questions:

+ What are we doing? - The goal or end result you're trying to achieve
+ Why are we doing it? - The reason or problem you're solving
+ How will we do it? - The specific steps or tasks needed to get there
+ Who's doing what? - Which people or teams are responsible for each part
+ When will it happen? - The timeline, including deadlines and milestones along the way
+ What will it cost? - The budget and resources needed
+ What could go wrong? - Potential risks and how you'll handle them

Think of it as the instruction manual for your project. Just like building furniture with IKEA instructions, a project plan shows you all the pieces you need, the order to put them together, and what the finished product should look like. It's the reference point everyone can look at to make sure they're on the same page and moving in the same direction.
It can be as simple as a few pages or as detailed as a thick binder, depending on how complex the project is. The key is that it captures enough information so that anyone involved knows what success looks like and how to get there.

## Purpose

Utilize the template below to properly document by way of explicit input or references to other artifacts those data elements necessary to ensure any team member can obtain an understanding of the project.  Minimally the Project Manager (PM) should be the author and maintainer of these data elements and perform a monthly review to:

+ Ensure all deliverables are being met.
+ Ensure all standards are being embraced using spot checks by direct inspection or delegation.
+ Update the Milestones and Timeline according to changes experienced per cycle / Sprint / iteration of work.

The PM should review the Project Plan with the customer on a bi-yearly basis to ensure understanding of all facets of the project are still apropos and that customer expectations are being met.

## Requirements/Specifications/Guidelines

A standard project plan typically requires the following core components:

**Project Overview**

+ Project title and description
+ Purpose and business justification
+ Clear objectives and success criteria
+ Scope (what's included and what's explicitly excluded)

**Stakeholders and Team**

+ Project sponsor and key stakeholders
+ Project manager identification
+ Team members and their roles/responsibilities
+ Communication plan (who needs what information, when, and how)

**Deliverables**

+ Specific outputs or products the project will create
+ Quality standards and acceptance criteria for each deliverable

**Schedule and Milestones**

+ Start and end dates
+ Major milestones (key checkpoints along the way)
+ Detailed task breakdown with durations
+ Dependencies (which tasks must be completed before others can start)

**Budget and Resources**

+ Cost estimates and budget allocation
+ Required resources (people, equipment, materials, technology)
+ Procurement needs

**Risk Management**

+ Identified potential risks
+ Likelihood and impact assessment
+ Mitigation strategies and contingency plans

**Quality Management**

+ Quality standards and metrics
+ Review and approval processes

**Change Management**

+ Process for handling changes to scope, schedule, or budget
+ Approval authority and documentation requirements

Most standards (like PMI's PMBOK, PRINCE2, or ISO 21500) require these elements as a minimum foundation. The level of detail and formality varies based on the organization's standards and the project's complexity.

## Measurable and Actionable

How a Project Plan is Measurable

+ Clear metrics and targets - Instead of saying "improve customer satisfaction," you'd say "achieve a customer satisfaction score of 85% or higher." You can measure whether you hit that number or not.

+ Defined milestones - These are concrete checkpoints you can mark as complete or incomplete. For example, "prototype approved by stakeholders" is something you can definitively say happened or didn't happen by a certain date.

+ Budget tracking - You know exactly how much you planned to spend versus how much you've actually spent at any point. If you budgeted $50,000 and you've spent $30,000, you can measure that you're at 60% of budget.

+ Schedule adherence - You can measure if tasks are on time, ahead, or behind schedule. If Task A was supposed to finish on March 15th and it's March 20th, you're measurably 5 days late.

+ Deliverable completion - Each deliverable has acceptance criteria, so you can measure whether it meets the requirements. Either the website has all the required features or it doesn't.

How a Project Plan is Actionable:

A project plan becomes actionable by breaking down big goals into specific steps that people can actually do:

+ Assigned responsibilities - Every task has a name attached to it. John knows he needs to draft the report, Sarah knows she needs to review it. They can take action because they know what's theirs to do.
+ Clear next steps - Instead of "launch product," the plan breaks it down into "conduct user testing," then "fix identified bugs," then "prepare marketing materials," then "schedule launch event." Each step is something concrete someone can start doing.
+ Sequenced tasks - The plan shows dependencies, so people know when they can begin their work. The designer can start creating mockups after requirements are finalized, not before.
+ Resource allocation - People know what tools, budget, or support they have access to. If the plan says you have $5,000 for software licenses, you can go purchase them.
+ Decision-making criteria - When issues arise, the plan provides guidelines for how to handle them, so people can take action without waiting for endless approvals.
+ Deadlines that drive behavior - Knowing that the client presentation is Friday means the team can work backward and take action today to prepare for it.

## Reference(s)

**Department of War (DoW)**

+DoW Instruction 5000.02: Operation of the Defense Acquisition System
+Defense Acquisition Guidebook - comprehensive project planning guidance
+Program Management Body of Knowledge (PM-BOK) for defense projects

**Office of Management and Budget (OMB)**

+ OMB Circular A-11: Preparation, Submission, and Execution of the Budget - includes capital planning and project management requirements
+ OMB Circular A-123: Management's Responsibility for Enterprise Risk Management and Internal Control
+ OMB Memoranda on IT project management and planning

**Government Accountability Office (GAO)**

+ GAO Schedule Assessment Guide (GAO-16-89G) - best practices for project scheduling
+ GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide (GAO-09-3SP) - comprehensive cost analysis for projects
+ GAO-12-375G: A Framework for Assessing the Reliability of Computer-Processed Data

**Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR)**

+ Part 7: Acquisition Planning - requirements for planning federal procurements and projects
+ Part 34: Major System Acquisition - specific project planning requirements for large systems
